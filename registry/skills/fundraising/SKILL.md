# Fundraising

Fundraising is a means, not a milestone. The goal is to get money to build the company, as fast as possible, with the least distraction. Every week spent fundraising is a week not spent building.

## When to raise

**Raise when:**
- You have evidence people want what you're building (usage, revenue, growth)
- You need capital to capture an opportunity that's time-sensitive
- You're default alive and raising extends your runway meaningfully

**Don't raise when:**
- You have nothing to show — investors are pattern-matching, and "just an idea" is the weakest position
- You're hoping money will solve a product problem (it won't)
- You're raising because other founders are raising

## Stage expectations

### Pre-seed ($250K–$1M)
- **What investors want:** A credible team with a specific insight about a real problem. Some evidence you've talked to users.
- **What you need:** A clear problem statement, early prototype or mockup, 10-20 user conversations that validate the problem exists.
- **Who invests:** Angels, pre-seed funds, YC and similar programs.

### Seed ($1M–$4M)
- **What investors want:** Product in market, early traction (users or revenue), evidence of PMF or clear path to it.
- **What you need:** Live product, some users, initial metrics showing retention or growth. A story about how this becomes a big company.
- **Who invests:** Seed funds, early-stage VCs, angels with larger checks.

### Series A ($5M–$15M)
- **What investors want:** PMF proven. Clear unit economics or path to them. Repeatable growth engine (not just founder hustle).
- **What you need:** Consistent month-over-month growth, retention curves that flatten, evidence the go-to-market motion works. $1M+ ARR for SaaS is common benchmark.
- **Who invests:** Series A VCs. They're looking for companies that can become very large.

## Round mechanics

### SAFEs (pre-seed, seed)

Simple Agreement for Future Equity. No board seat, no complex terms. Standard YC SAFE.

- **Valuation cap:** The maximum valuation at which your SAFE converts. Lower cap = more dilution for you.
- **Discount:** Alternative to cap. Investor gets X% discount on next round's price.
- **Post-money vs pre-money SAFEs:** YC's standard is post-money. This means you know exactly how much you're diluting. Use it.
- **Pro-rata rights:** Right to invest in future rounds. Standard for lead investors. Fine to give.

**Key rule:** Don't negotiate SAFE terms beyond the cap. The whole point is simplicity. If an investor wants custom terms on a SAFE, that's a yellow flag.

### Priced rounds (Series A+)

Preferred stock with actual terms. You need a lawyer.

- **Lead investor:** Sets terms, does diligence, writes the biggest check. You need one — don't try to cobble together a round without a lead.
- **Board seat:** Series A lead typically gets one. 2-1 or 2-1-1 (founders-investor or founders-investor-independent) is standard.
- **Liquidation preference:** 1x non-participating is standard. Anything more is a bad sign.
- **Anti-dilution:** Broad-based weighted average is standard. Full ratchet is aggressive — push back.

## Running the process

### Creating leverage

Fundraising works when investors feel urgency. Urgency comes from:

1. **Parallel conversations.** Talk to 20-30 investors in the same 2-week window. Not serially.
2. **Social proof.** Other credible investors being interested. Named angels who've committed.
3. **Business momentum.** Metrics that are moving up and to the right *during* the raise.

### The pitch

- **Open with what you do.** One sentence. No buildup.
- **Show the problem with specifics.** A real user, a real situation, real numbers on how bad the current solution is.
- **Demo or metrics.** Show, don't tell. If you have a product, demo it. If you have traction, show the graph.
- **Market size.** Bottom-up, not top-down. "There are 50,000 companies like our first 10 customers, they'd each pay $20K/year" > "The market is $50B."
- **Team.** Why you specifically are the right people to build this. Ideally: founder-market fit, relevant experience, or proof you can execute.
- **The ask.** How much you're raising, on what terms, and what you'll do with it.

### What investors are actually evaluating

In this order:
1. Is this a big market?
2. Is there evidence people want this?
3. Can this team execute?
4. What's the insight that others are missing?

They will not tell you what they're evaluating. They'll ask about your go-to-market or your technical architecture. But the real question is always one of the four above.

### Handling "no"

Most investors will say no. Many won't say it clearly — they'll say "keep us updated" or "we'd love to see more traction." These mean no.

**Real interest looks like:** Scheduling a follow-up quickly, introducing you to their partners, asking for data room access, discussing terms.

Don't waste time on maybes. Move on to the next conversation.

## Common mistakes

- **Raising too much.** More money = higher expectations = less time. Raise what you need for 18-24 months.
- **Optimizing for valuation.** A high valuation with a bad investor is worse than a lower valuation with the right partner.
- **Taking too long.** Set a 2-3 week window. If you can't close in that window, pause and go build more.
- **Not having a plan for the money.** "We'll figure it out" doesn't work. Know what milestones the money gets you to.
- **Treating fundraising as validation.** Investors being excited ≠ PMF. The market validates you, not VCs.
