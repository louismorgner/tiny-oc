# People

The hardest part of building a company isn't the product or the market — it's the people. Most founder mistakes in this area come from avoiding hard conversations or copying practices from big companies that don't apply to a 5-person startup.

## Hiring

### Early hires (first 10)

Your first hires are not "filling roles." They're choosing who you'll spend the next 5 years with in a high-pressure environment.

**Optimize for:**
- **Agency.** Will they figure things out without being told what to do? Ask: "Tell me about something you built or did where nobody asked you to."
- **Speed.** Do they ship? Or do they plan, discuss, and optimize forever?
- **Adaptability.** In 6 months, their job will look different. Can they handle ambiguity?
- **Taste.** Do they care about quality? Not perfection — quality. Do they know the difference?

**Don't optimize for:**
- Pedigree (big company names, prestigious schools)
- Years of experience (relevant experience matters, years don't)
- Culture "fit" (this often means "people like me" — optimize for culture *add*)

### How to interview

- **Work on a real problem together.** Give them an actual problem you're facing. Spend 60-90 minutes working on it. You'll learn more than any behavioral interview.
- **Check references aggressively.** Don't just call the people they listed. Find back-channel references — people who worked with them that they didn't suggest. Ask: "Would you hire this person again?" and listen to the hesitation.
- **Move fast.** Good people have options. If you like someone, decide in days, not weeks.

### When to hire

- Only when the pain of *not* hiring is acute and specific
- When you've tried to solve the problem without hiring and can't
- Never to "get ahead of growth" — hire behind demand, not in front of it

## Firing

The single hardest thing founders do. Also the thing they wait too long to do, almost every time.

### When it's time

- You've given clear feedback and reasonable time (2-4 weeks, not 6 months), and things haven't changed
- You find yourself working around the person — assigning their work to others, avoiding giving them important projects
- The rest of the team knows this person isn't performing (they always know)
- Your gut has been telling you for weeks, but you keep finding reasons to wait

### How to do it

- **Be direct.** "This isn't working out, and we're letting you go." Not "we're restructuring" or "the role is changing."
- **Be kind.** This is someone's livelihood. Treat them with dignity. Offer reasonable severance. Help with the transition.
- **Be fast.** Once you've decided, do it within days. Every day you wait makes it worse for everyone, including the person being let go.
- **Don't apologize endlessly.** One genuine expression of empathy. Then move to logistics.

### After

- Tell the team directly and promptly. Don't let rumors fill the void.
- Be honest but respectful: "X is leaving the company. We wish them well." Don't badmouth.
- Reflect on what went wrong. Did you hire wrong, manage wrong, or was it just a mismatch?

## Giving feedback

### The core framework

From Kim Scott's Radical Candor: **Care personally, challenge directly.** Most founders do one or the other. Good feedback does both simultaneously.

- **Ruinous empathy:** You care but don't challenge. "Everything's fine" when it isn't. The person never improves and is eventually fired, blindsided.
- **Obnoxious aggression:** You challenge but don't care. Feedback is harsh, personal, or delivered in a way that shuts people down.
- **Radical candor:** "I'm telling you this because I care about your growth and the company. [Specific behavior] is causing [specific impact]. Here's what I need to see instead."

### Delivering hard feedback

1. **Be specific.** Not "your work quality is slipping." Instead: "The last two PRs had bugs that broke production. That's not the bar we need."
2. **Focus on behavior, not character.** "You interrupted Sarah three times in the meeting" vs "You're rude."
3. **Do it promptly.** Feedback is stale after a week. Give it within 24 hours.
4. **Do it privately.** Never criticize in front of the team.
5. **Make it a conversation.** Ask for their perspective. Sometimes there's context you're missing.

## Conflict resolution

### Cofounder disagreements

The most dangerous conflicts. Most cofounder breakups could've been prevented by having explicit conversations early.

**Prevent:**
- Agree on decision-making authority by domain. Who has final say on product? Engineering? Business?
- Talk about the hard scenarios *before* they happen: What if one of us wants to leave? What if we disagree on a pivot? What if one person isn't pulling their weight?
- Have regular (weekly) honest check-ins. Not about tasks — about how the relationship is working.

**Resolve:**
- Name the disagreement explicitly. "We disagree about X. Let's work through it."
- Separate the decision from the relationship. You can disagree and still respect each other.
- Use data where possible. "Let's test both approaches for 2 weeks and look at the results."
- If you're stuck, bring in a trusted third party (advisor, board member). Not to decide — to help you think.

### Team conflicts

- Address early. Small tensions become big resentments fast.
- Talk to each person individually first. Understand their perspective before mediating.
- Focus on the behavior and the impact, not who's "right."
- Sometimes people just don't work well together. Restructuring is better than forcing a relationship.

## Leading a small team

### Context over control

Don't tell people what to do. Tell them what matters and why, and let them figure out the how. This means:
- Share the strategy, the metrics, the constraints
- Explain the "why" behind decisions
- Let people own their area completely
- Intervene only when the outcome matters more than their learning

### 1:1s

The most important meeting a manager has. Weekly, 30 minutes, never skip.

**Questions that surface real problems:**
- "What's the biggest thing slowing you down?"
- "Is there anything you'd change about how we work?"
- "What's something I should know but might not?"
- "Are you working on what you think is most important?"

**Don't** turn 1:1s into status updates. You can get status from Slack.

### Decision-making

- **DRI (Directly Responsible Individual).** Every decision has one person who decides. Not consensus. Not committee. One person.
- **Disagree and commit.** Once the DRI decides, everyone commits. Relitigate only if new information emerges.
- **Reversible decisions fast, irreversible decisions slow.** Most decisions are reversible. Move.
