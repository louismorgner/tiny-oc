# Positioning

Positioning is not a tagline. It's the strategic decision about how your product fits in the customer's world. Get it right and everything downstream — messaging, sales, marketing, pricing — gets easier. Get it wrong and you're constantly explaining yourself.

The core framework here is from April Dunford (Obviously Awesome), adapted for startups.

## The five components

### 1. Competitive alternatives

What would your customers do if you didn't exist?

This is NOT "competitors." It's whatever the customer currently does to solve the problem — including doing nothing, using spreadsheets, hiring an intern, or building something in-house.

**Why it matters:** Your positioning is always relative to the alternative. If customers compare you to enterprise software, you need different positioning than if they compare you to a spreadsheet.

### 2. Unique attributes

What do you have that the alternatives don't?

Be honest. Only list things that are genuinely different, not "better UI" (everyone says that). These are features, capabilities, or properties that the alternatives literally cannot claim.

### 3. Value

What do those unique attributes actually enable for the customer?

Map each attribute to a concrete outcome. "Real-time sync" → "Your team never works on stale data." Attributes are features. Value is what those features mean for the customer's life.

### 4. Target customers

Who cares most about that value?

Not "everyone." Not "SMBs." The specific type of customer for whom your unique value is most important. The tighter this is, the stronger your positioning.

**Good:** "Series A SaaS companies with 10-50 engineers who just hired their first platform team."
**Bad:** "Companies that want to move faster."

### 5. Market category

What type of thing are you?

This sets expectations. If you say "CRM," people immediately know roughly what you do, who uses it, and what it costs. If you say "collaboration platform," you've said nothing.

**Three options:**
- **Existing category:** You're a better X. Works when the category is well-understood and you have clear differentiation.
- **Subcategory:** You're X for Y. Works when you serve a specific segment much better than the general solution.
- **New category:** You're something that didn't exist before. Only works if you can educate the market (expensive and slow). Most startups shouldn't try this.

## How to do it

### Step 1: Start with your best customers

Don't position for the market. Position for the people who already love you. Who are your happiest, most engaged users? What do they have in common?

### Step 2: Work through the five components in order

Go in this exact order: alternatives → attributes → value → customers → category. Each step builds on the previous one.

### Step 3: Test it

Your positioning should pass three tests:

1. **A stranger can explain what you do after reading your homepage for 10 seconds.** If they can't, your positioning is unclear.
2. **Your best customers would nod and say "yes, that's why I use it."** If they'd say "well, kind of, but actually I use it because..." then you've positioned wrong.
3. **Your sales team can use it.** If the positioning doesn't translate into a sales pitch that wins deals, it's academic.

## When to reposition

- Your fastest-growing segment isn't who you originally built for
- Customers consistently describe you differently than you describe yourself
- You keep losing deals to a competitor you thought was irrelevant
- Your category is dying or becoming commoditized

## Common mistakes

- **Positioning by aspiration.** You position for the market you want to be in, not the one you're actually in. Be honest about where you are.
- **Being everything to everyone.** Weak positioning tries not to exclude anyone. Strong positioning deliberately excludes to be compelling to the right people.
- **Category creation without the resources.** Creating a new category requires enormous marketing spend and time. Unless you're well-funded and patient, pick an existing category and differentiate within it.
- **Confusing positioning with messaging.** Positioning is the strategic choice. Messaging is how you express it. Get the positioning right first, then write the copy.
