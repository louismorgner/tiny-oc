# Pricing

Most founders underprice. Some overcomplicate. Few get it right early. The good news: pricing is one of the most reversible decisions you'll make, so move fast and iterate.

## First principles

### Price on value, not cost

Your price should reflect the value the customer gets, not what it costs you to deliver. If your product saves a company $100K/year, charging $1K is leaving $99K on the table. If it saves them $500/year, charging $5K won't work no matter how good it is.

**How to estimate value:**
- What does the customer pay for the current solution (including time, labor, other tools)?
- What is the cost of *not* solving this problem?
- How much revenue/savings does your product generate for them?

A reasonable starting point: charge 10-20% of the value you create.

### Price is a signal

- Too cheap signals "toy" or "not serious"
- Too expensive creates objections that slow sales
- Free removes all signal — you learn nothing about willingness to pay

Charge from day one if you can. Even $1/month tells you more than free.

## Packaging

### Good/better/best (3 tiers)

The most common and effective structure. Works because:
- Gives customers choice without overwhelming them
- The middle tier is the anchor — most people pick it
- The top tier makes the middle feel reasonable

**How to split tiers:**
- **Starter:** Core value only. Low price, attracts volume, low-touch.
- **Pro/Team:** Full feature set. This is your main revenue driver.
- **Enterprise:** Pro + compliance, SSO, SLAs, dedicated support. Custom pricing.

### Per-seat vs usage-based

**Per-seat** is simple and predictable. Works when value scales with team size (collaboration tools, CRMs).

**Usage-based** aligns price with value. Works when usage varies wildly between customers (APIs, infrastructure, data tools). Harder to forecast revenue.

**Hybrid** (base fee + usage) captures the benefits of both. Increasingly common.

### What to gate

Gate features that differentiate your tiers. Never gate core value — if the free/cheap tier doesn't deliver the core product experience, people won't upgrade because they'll never see the value.

## Setting the initial price

### For B2B SaaS

1. Talk to 5-10 potential customers. Ask about the problem, not the price.
2. Propose a price that feels slightly uncomfortable (too high).
3. If nobody pushes back, you're too cheap. Raise it.
4. If everyone says no immediately, you're too expensive or the value isn't clear.
5. You want ~20-30% of prospects to say "that's more than I expected" but still buy.

### For consumer/PLG

1. Look at what adjacent products charge. Customers have anchor prices in their head.
2. Start with a free tier that demonstrates value + a paid tier that removes a real friction.
3. Optimize the conversion funnel before optimizing the price.

## When to raise prices

- When you're closing too easily (>80% win rate means you're underpriced)
- When customers say "this is way too cheap for what we get"
- When you add significant new value
- When you move upmarket

**How to raise:**
- Grandfather existing customers (at least for a period)
- Announce clearly, with reasoning
- New customers get new pricing immediately
- Don't apologize — if you're delivering more value, the price should reflect it

## Common mistakes

- **Competing on price.** Unless you have structural cost advantages, price competition is a race to the bottom. Compete on value.
- **Too many tiers.** Three is almost always right. Five is too many. One is too few (no anchor).
- **Pricing by committee.** Don't survey 100 customers on what they'd pay. They'll all say less. Price based on value, test with real sales.
- **Free tier too generous.** If customers can get everything they need for free, they will. The free tier should create desire for paid, not satisfy all needs.
- **Annual vs monthly.** Offer both. Discount annual by 15-20%. Annual is better for cash flow and retention. But monthly lowers the barrier to start.
- **Discounting.** Occasional strategic discounts are fine (annual prepay, early adopter). Habitual discounting trains customers to wait and destroys your pricing power.
