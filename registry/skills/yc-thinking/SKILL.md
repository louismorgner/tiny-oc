# YC Thinking

The best startup advice is a small set of ideas applied relentlessly. This skill encodes the core frameworks from YC, Paul Graham, and the partners who've seen thousands of startups.

Use this as a thinking foundation. Not to parrot advice — to reason clearly about the specific situation using proven mental models.

## Core ideas

### Make something people want

The only thing that matters pre-PMF. Not "build something cool" or "disrupt an industry." Find people with a problem, make the thing that solves it, and verify they actually want it by measuring whether they use it and pay for it.

**How to tell if people want it:**
- They use it without being asked
- They complain when it breaks
- They tell other people about it (unprompted)
- They pay for it, or clearly would

**How to tell if they don't:**
- You have to convince people to try it
- Usage drops off after first week
- People say "that's cool" but don't come back
- You're spending more on acquisition than the product generates

### Do things that don't scale

At the beginning, do the manual, unscalable thing. Recruit users one at a time. Onboard them personally. Do the work your software will eventually do. This is how you learn what to build and build something people love.

Founders resist this because it feels wrong — it doesn't feel like building a "startup." But every great company did this. Stripe installed their payment system on people's laptops. Airbnb went door to door photographing apartments.

The danger is doing only scalable things before you've earned the right to scale.

### Talk to users

Not surveys. Not analytics. Actual conversations.

**Good user research (The Mom Test):**
- Ask about their life, not your idea
- Ask about specifics in the past, not generics about the future
- Talk less, listen more
- "Tell me about the last time you had this problem" > "Would you use a product that..."
- Bad data: "Yeah I'd definitely use that!" Good data: "Last Tuesday I spent 3 hours doing X because..."

### Launch now

Your launch is not a product launch with press coverage. It's the moment you put your thing in front of real users. Do it as early as possible. You will learn more from one week of real usage than six months of building.

Waiting to launch is almost always fear disguised as perfectionism.

### Startup = Growth

A startup is a company designed to grow fast. That's it. Not "a small company" or "a tech company." Growth rate is the metric that tells you if you're on track.

**Good weekly growth rates:**
- 5-7% week-over-week is good
- 10%+ is exceptional
- 1% means something is fundamentally wrong with the product or market

### Default alive or default dead

Take your current revenue, growth rate, and expenses. If you keep going at this rate, do you get to profitability before you run out of money? If yes, you're default alive. If no, you're default dead.

Default dead companies need to either cut costs or raise money. The founder should know which one they are at all times.

### Ramen profitable

The point where revenue covers the founders' basic living expenses. Powerful because it means you control your own destiny — you don't need anyone's permission to keep going. Getting here fast changes the entire dynamic of the company.

### Don't scale prematurely

The #1 startup killer (after building something nobody wants). Premature scaling means:
- Hiring before you have PMF
- Spending on marketing before you have retention
- Building infrastructure for millions of users when you have hundreds
- "Professionalizing" with processes, meetings, and org charts when you have 5 people

### Morale is real

Startups are emotional. The founder's state directly affects the company. Low morale kills startups not because people feel bad but because they stop making good decisions and stop doing the hard things.

Things that help: shipping fast, talking to happy users, small wins, cutting scope to finish things. Things that hurt: long projects with no milestones, features nobody uses, vague strategy conversations.

## Decision frameworks

### Reversible vs irreversible

Most decisions are reversible (pricing, feature choices, marketing channels). Make them fast. A few are irreversible (co-founder splits, fundraising terms, key hires). Slow down for these.

Founders waste enormous time agonizing over reversible decisions.

### The schlep filter

The best startup ideas often involve doing hard, unpleasant work that others avoid. If an idea makes you think "that sounds painful" — that's the schlep filter talking. The pain is what keeps competitors out.

### Tarpit ideas

Ideas that seem great, attract many founders, but almost never work. Social apps for X niche, marketplace for Y, AI wrapper around Z. They look good because they're easy to imagine. They fail because the execution challenges are invisible from the outside.

Warning signs: many people have tried it, the problem sounds universal but behavior change is required, network effects need critical mass.

### Organic vs paid growth

If your product requires paid acquisition to grow, that's a red flag pre-PMF. Organic growth (word of mouth, SEO, content, viral loops) is a signal that people actually want what you're building. Paid growth is for accelerating something that's already working.

## How to use this skill

When a founder brings a question, check if it maps to one of these core ideas. Often the answer is simpler than they think — they just need to talk to users, ship faster, or stop doing things that don't matter.

But don't be a broken record. Apply the thinking, don't recite the advice. The founder needs to hear "based on what you're telling me, your churn suggests people aren't getting value from the onboarding — have you watched someone go through it?" not "well, YC says to talk to users."
